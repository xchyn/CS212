1.Where (0, 0) is

For the last four lectures you have been an assembler of parts. You created a JFrame, you put components into it, you handed the arrangement over to a layout manager, and you registered listeners so that clicks ran your code. At no point did you decide what any pixel looked like. A JButton drew itself; a JLabel drew itself; you never asked how.

Today you take that job. Everything from here on is about one object — a Graphics — and about roughly a dozen methods you call on it. The concepts are small. The place people lose marks is the very first one, so we start there and we are going to be pedantic about it.

Definition— the graphics coordinate system

Every drawing method takes positions measured in pixels from the top-left corner of the component you are drawing on. That corner is (0, 0), and it is called the origin.

  • x increases to the right — the same as in mathematics.
  • y increases downward — the opposite of mathematics.

The coordinates are relative to the component, not the screen and not the frame. In a JPanel that sits inside a frame, the panel's own top-left corner is (0, 0) no matter where the window is dragged to.

Key Idea— bigger y means lower down

In your maths classes, a point with a larger y is higher on the page. In computer graphics, a point with a larger y is further down the screen. The point (100, 300) sits below the point (100, 50).

This is not Java being awkward. It is the way essentially every screen and every printer has worked since the 1960s: an image is drawn as a series of horizontal lines starting at the top, so counting rows downward from zero is the natural thing to do. Java, C#, HTML canvases, iOS and Android all do it.

Here are the two conventions side by side. Read the arrow directions, not the labels.

Worked Example— which way does the diagonal go?

A BufferedImage is a rectangle of pixels in memory. Calling createGraphics() on one gives you a Graphics that draws into it, and getRGB(x, y) afterwards tells you exactly what landed where. Predict all four answers before you reveal: the line runs from (0, 0) to (200, 200), so which corners of the image does it pass through?

WhichWayIsUp.java

import java.awt.Color;
import java.awt.Graphics;
import java.awt.image.BufferedImage;

// Draws the line from (0,0) to (200,200) and asks the image where the ink is.
public class WhichWayIsUp {

   public static void main(String args[]) {
      BufferedImage sheet = new BufferedImage(201, 201, BufferedImage.TYPE_INT_RGB);
      Graphics g = sheet.createGraphics();

      g.setColor(Color.WHITE);
      g.fillRect(0, 0, 201, 201);

      g.setColor(Color.BLACK);
      g.drawLine(0, 0, 200, 200);

      System.out.println("drawLine(0, 0, 200, 200)");
      System.out.println("   ink at (10, 10),  near the TOP-left corner:    " + inked(sheet, 10, 10));
      System.out.println("   ink at (10, 190), near the BOTTOM-left corner: " + inked(sheet, 10, 190));
      System.out.println("   ink at (190, 190), near the BOTTOM-right:      " + inked(sheet, 190, 190));
      System.out.println("   ink at (190, 10),  near the TOP-right:         " + inked(sheet, 190, 10));
   }

   static boolean inked(BufferedImage sheet, int x, int y) {
      return (sheet.getRGB(x, y) & 0xFFFFFF) != 0xFFFFFF;
   }
}

The line goes from the top-left corner down to the bottom-right corner. In a maths class the line y = x climbs from bottom-left to top-right; on a screen the identical pair of numbers descends. If you remember one image from this lecture, make it this one.

Two smaller things worth banking now, because both come back later:

  • You can draw without a window. Nothing in this program mentions JFrame, JPanel or setVisible. Graphics is not tied to the screen — the same methods draw into a window, into an image, or onto a printer. That is why we can check our arithmetic exactly rather than squinting at a picture.
  • 0xFFFFFF is white. getRGB hands back one int holding all three color channels packed together; masking off the top byte leaves red, green and blue, and all three at maximum is white. §4 unpacks that number properly.
Quick Check 19.1

A JPanel is 400 pixels wide and 300 pixels tall.

(a) What are the coordinates of its four corners?

Top-left (0, 0), top-right (399, 0), bottom-left (0, 299), bottom-right (399, 299).

The largest legal x is width - 1, not width, for exactly the same reason the last index of a 400-element array is 399. It is worth saying out loud because the drawing methods themselves are happy to accept 400, or 4000, or −20 — nothing is checked, and anything off the edge is simply not shown.

(b) What is the coordinate of the exact center?

(200, 150) — that is getWidth() / 2 and getHeight() / 2. Writing it with those two methods rather than with the literals 200 and 150 is what makes a drawing stay centered when the user resizes the window.

(c) You want a dot 60 pixels above the center. What y do you use?

y = 150 - 60, which is 90. Above means subtract. This is the part that catches people: "up" is the direction of decreasing y, so moving up the screen means taking numbers away.

Things to notice across all three parts. Whenever a question says above, higher, up or the top of, translate it into "smaller y" before you write any arithmetic. Doing that translation on paper, once, prevents the single most common sign error in graphics code.

Quick Check 19.2

Two windows are on the screen. Window A is at the top-left of the display; window B has been dragged to the bottom-right. Each contains a JPanel that draws a dot at (10, 10). Where does each dot appear, and are the two Graphics calls different?

Plan. Ask what (0, 0) means for the object being drawn on.

Each dot appears 10 pixels right and 10 pixels down from the top-left corner of its own panel. On the screen as a whole the two dots are far apart, but the code is identical and neither panel knows or cares where its window has been dragged to.

Things to notice. Coordinates are always component-local. This is why a panel's drawing code never mentions the window position and never breaks when the window moves. It is also why, when you nest a panel inside another panel, the inner one starts counting from its own corner again — Swing translates the Graphics for you before it calls your code.

2.paintComponent: the method you write and never call

So you want to draw. Where does the code go? Not in main, and not in a button's actionPerformed — we will see in §6 exactly why that fails. It goes in one specific method, on a class of your own that extends JPanel.

Definition— the Graphics object

Graphics, from java.awt, is Java's drawing context: an object that knows what surface is being drawn on, and remembers a current color and a current font. Every drawing operation is a method call on a Graphics.

You never construct one with new. Java hands you a Graphics when it is ready for you to draw — and the standard way it does that is by calling your paintComponent method and passing one in.

Graphics2D extends Graphics with extra abilities: line thickness (setStroke), transparency, gradients, rotation. The object Swing hands you is always really a Graphics2D, so Graphics2D g2 = (Graphics2D) g; is a cast that always succeeds — an ordinary downcast of exactly the kind you met in Lecture 13.

Definition— paintComponent

protected void paintComponent(Graphics g) is the method every Swing component uses to draw its own content. To draw your own picture you write a class that extends JPanel and overrides it:

public class MyPanel extends JPanel {
   public void paintComponent(Graphics g) {
      super.paintComponent(g);
      // your drawing goes here
   }
}

Three rules govern it, and all three are exam material:

  1. You override it; you never call it. Swing calls it, whenever the component needs to be on the screen.
  2. super.paintComponent(g); is the first line — it fills the component with its background color, wiping out whatever was drawn last time.
  3. It must be prepared to run at any moment, any number of times. You do not control when it happens, so it has to redraw the whole picture from scratch every time.

Rule 3 is the one that reshapes how you think. paintComponent is not a command that puts something on the screen once. It is your answer to a question Swing asks over and over: what does this component look like right now?

Procedure— when Swing calls paintComponent

You never call it. Swing does, when any of these happens:

  1. The component is displayed for the first time.
  2. The window is resized, minimised and restored, or moved partly off the screen and back.
  3. Another window that was covering it is moved away.
  4. Your program calls repaint() — the subject of §5.

The first three are outside your control entirely. That is why the method must be able to reproduce the entire picture using only what the object currently knows.

Common Mistake— leaving out super.paintComponent(g)

super.paintComponent(g) is what erases the previous frame. Leave it out and the old ink stays on the component, and every new drawing is painted on top of the last one. On a static picture you may never notice. The moment anything moves, you get a smear.

The next worked example proves it, pixel by pixel.

Worked Example— what the super call is actually for

Two panels, identical except for one line. Each draws a single blue circle at a position held in a field. The driver paints each panel twice onto the same image — once with the circle at the left, then again with it moved to the right — which is exactly what Swing does to a component whose contents have changed.

Before you reveal: for each panel, is there blue ink left at the old position?

SuperTest.java

import java.awt.Color;
import java.awt.Graphics;
import java.awt.Graphics2D;
import java.awt.image.BufferedImage;

import javax.swing.JPanel;

// A panel that draws one filled circle. Where it goes is decided by a field,
// so we can move the circle and paint the panel a second time.
abstract class CirclePanel extends JPanel {
   int x = 50;
}

class GoodPanel extends CirclePanel {
   protected void paintComponent(Graphics g) {
      super.paintComponent(g);        // clears the panel to its background color
      g.setColor(Color.BLUE);
      g.fillOval(x, 50, 100, 100);
   }
}

class BadPanel extends CirclePanel {
   protected void paintComponent(Graphics g) {
      g.setColor(Color.BLUE);         // the super call has been left out
      g.fillOval(x, 50, 100, 100);
   }
}

public class SuperTest {

   // Is there blue ink at this point of the image?
   static boolean blueAt(BufferedImage img, int x, int y) {
      return (img.getRGB(x, y) & 0xFFFFFF) == 0x0000FF;
   }

   static void paintTwice(String name, CirclePanel p) {
      p.setSize(400, 200);
      p.setBackground(Color.WHITE);

      BufferedImage img = new BufferedImage(400, 200, BufferedImage.TYPE_INT_RGB);
      Graphics2D g = img.createGraphics();

      p.x = 50;
      p.paint(g);          // first paint:  circle centered at (100, 100)
      p.x = 250;
      p.paint(g);          // second paint: circle centered at (300, 100)

      g.dispose();

      System.out.println(name);
      System.out.println("   ink at the OLD position (100,100): " + blueAt(img, 100, 100));
      System.out.println("   ink at the NEW position (300,100): " + blueAt(img, 300, 100));
   }

   public static void main(String args[]) {
      paintTwice("GoodPanel -- calls super.paintComponent(g)", new GoodPanel());
      paintTwice("BadPanel  -- leaves it out", new BadPanel());
   }
}
GoodPanel -- calls super.paintComponent(g)
   ink at the OLD position (100,100): false
   ink at the NEW position (300,100): true
BadPanel  -- leaves it out
   ink at the OLD position (100,100): true
   ink at the NEW position (300,100): true

There it is, with no room for argument. GoodPanel shows one circle: the super call filled the whole panel with white before redrawing, so the old circle is gone. BadPanel shows two circles — the object only ever wanted to draw one, but the first one was never erased.

In a real animation, dragging a shape across a BadPanel leaves a solid trail behind it. Students usually describe this as "my program is drawing too much"; it is not. It is drawing exactly the right thing and failing to remove the previous frame.

Two details in the harness are worth naming.

  • p.paint(g) rather than p.paintComponent(g). paint is the public entry point Swing itself uses; it runs paintComponent, then the border, then any child components. paintComponent is protected, which is a deliberate signal: it is not part of the public interface, because you are not supposed to call it.
  • CirclePanel is an abstract class holding the shared field x, so one helper method can take either panel. Straight out of Lecture 14 — and it will be the shape of the whole design by §8.
Quick Check 19.3

A student's panel draws a clock face. It looks perfect when the program starts. They then drag another window across it, and afterwards half the clock is missing.

Plan. Something wiped part of the component and it was not redrawn. Ask who was supposed to redraw it.

Almost certainly the drawing is not in paintComponent at all — it was done once, somewhere else, straight after the window appeared. Covering a component destroys its pixels; Swing recovers them by calling paintComponent again, and if the drawing code is not in there, there is nothing to recover.

Things to notice. "It looked right until something covered it" is the signature symptom of drawing outside paintComponent, and §6 shows the exact code that produces it. The cure is always the same: move the drawing into paintComponent, and keep whatever data it needs in fields of the panel.

A second, rarer cause fits the same symptom: paintComponent that draws different things on different calls — for instance one that reads from a Scanner, or that uses Math.random() without storing the result. A repaint must be able to reproduce the same picture, so anything random has to be generated once and remembered.

Quick Check 19.4

Answer briefly.

(a) Why is paintComponent declared protected rather than public?

Because it is meant to be overridden, not called. protected lets a subclass — yours — supply a new body, while keeping the method out of the public interface so that other code cannot invoke it directly. The access modifier is documentation: it says "this is a hook, not a service".

You may legally widen it to public in your override, and plenty of code does, including your instructor's PaintingPanel in §7. That is allowed — an override may widen access, never narrow it — but it does not make calling it a good idea.

(b) What is the type of the parameter g, and where did the object come from?

Its declared type is Graphics. The object Swing actually passes is a Graphics2D — a subclass — which is why the downcast (Graphics2D) g never fails. Swing created it, aimed at the right piece of the screen, and it is only valid for the duration of this one call, which is another reason not to save it in a field and use it later.

Things to notice across both parts. Static type Graphics, dynamic type Graphics2D: the exact situation from Lecture 13. The declared type limits what you can call without a cast; the real object decides what actually happens.

Key Idea— an oval is described by the box around it

drawOval(x, y, w, h) does not take a center and a radius. It takes the bounding box: the invisible rectangle the ellipse is inscribed in, given exactly the way drawRect gives one — top-left corner, then width, then height.

So drawOval(50, 220, 150, 100) produces an ellipse 150 wide and 100 tall whose leftmost point is at x = 50 and whose topmost point is at y = 220. Its center is at (50 + 75, 220 + 50) = (125, 270), and nothing in the call says so.

To draw a circle of diameter d centered at (cx, cy) you must do the arithmetic yourself:

g.fillOval(cx - d / 2, cy - d / 2, d, d);

Forgetting this is, by a wide margin, the second most common error in beginner graphics code — after getting y upside down.

Worked Example— measuring exactly where the ink goes

The table above says "top-left corner, width, height", but there is a subtlety it hides. Does drawRect(50, 60, 150, 100) ink 150 columns, or 151? Rather than argue about it, draw each shape on a blank image and find the smallest rectangle containing every colored pixel.

Predict the five answers before you reveal. In particular: for drawString("Line", 125, 70), is the text above the line y = 70, below it, or centered on it?

WhereIsTheInk.java

import java.awt.Color;
import java.awt.Graphics;
import java.awt.image.BufferedImage;

// Draws one shape on a blank white image and reports exactly which pixels
// ended up colored. Nothing is displayed; there is no window and no JFrame.
public class WhereIsTheInk {

   static BufferedImage sheet;
   static Graphics pen;

   // A fresh 400 x 400 white image, with the pen already set to black.
   static void blankSheet() {
      sheet = new BufferedImage(400, 400, BufferedImage.TYPE_INT_RGB);
      pen = sheet.createGraphics();       // a Graphics, exactly like paintComponent's
      pen.setColor(Color.WHITE);
      pen.fillRect(0, 0, 400, 400);
      pen.setColor(Color.BLACK);
   }

   // The smallest rectangle that contains every non-white pixel.
   static void report(String call) {
      int minX = 400, minY = 400, maxX = -1, maxY = -1;
      for (int y = 0; y < 400; y++) {
         for (int x = 0; x < 400; x++) {
            if ((sheet.getRGB(x, y) & 0xFFFFFF) != 0xFFFFFF) {
               if (x < minX) minX = x;
               if (x > maxX) maxX = x;
               if (y < minY) minY = y;
               if (y > maxY) maxY = y;
            }
         }
      }
      System.out.println(call);
      System.out.println("      x from " + minX + " to " + maxX + "  (" + (maxX - minX + 1) + " pixels wide)");
      System.out.println("      y from " + minY + " to " + maxY + "  (" + (maxY - minY + 1) + " pixels tall)");
   }

   public static void main(String args[]) {
      blankSheet();
      pen.drawRect(50, 60, 150, 100);
      report("drawRect(50, 60, 150, 100)");

      blankSheet();
      pen.fillRect(50, 60, 150, 100);
      report("fillRect(50, 60, 150, 100)");

      blankSheet();
      pen.drawOval(50, 60, 150, 100);
      report("drawOval(50, 60, 150, 100)");

      blankSheet();
      pen.fillOval(50, 60, 150, 100);
      report("fillOval(50, 60, 150, 100)");

      blankSheet();
      pen.drawString("Line", 125, 70);
      report("drawString(\"Line\", 125, 70)");
   }
}

Four facts fall straight out of those numbers.

  • The bounding box is real. drawOval(50, 60, 150, 100) inks exactly the same rectangle of the image as drawRect(50, 60, 150, 100): x from 50, y from 60. The oval touches all four sides of that box from the inside and goes nowhere near the corners.
  • An outline is one pixel wider than a fill. drawRect spans 151 columns, fillRect spans 150. The outline is drawn on the boundary, so it includes both x = 50 and x = 50 + 150; the fill covers the interior, from x = 50 up to but not including x = 200. In practice you can ignore the difference; on a "what exactly is at pixel (200, 60)?" exam question you cannot.
  • The y in drawString is the baseline, not the top. The text was drawn at y = 70 and its ink lies at y 61 to 69 — entirely above the number you gave. That is the line the letters sit on, the same baseline you write on with lined paper; descenders on letters like g and p drop below it. Ask for text at y = 5 in a large font and it disappears off the top of the panel, which mystifies everybody the first time.
  • Text size depends on the font. The 23-by-9 figure is for whatever default font this machine has installed; a different machine or a different look and feel will give a slightly different number. The baseline rule does not vary.

Two of the methods have arguments that are not obvious even once you know about bounding boxes. Both appear in your instructor's demo program, so pin them down now.

Definition— drawRoundRect and drawArc

drawRoundRect(x, y, w, h, arcWidth, arcHeight) draws the same rectangle as drawRect, but each corner is replaced by a quarter-ellipse. arcWidth and arcHeight are the full width and height of the ellipse the corner is cut from, so drawRoundRect(50, 60, 150, 100, 40, 40) rounds each corner with a quarter-circle of radius 20. Passing 0 for both gives you an ordinary rectangle.

drawArc(x, y, w, h, startAngle, arcAngle) draws part of the ellipse inscribed in that bounding box. Both angles are in degrees:

  • startAngle = 0 is the 3 o'clock position — the rightmost point of the ellipse.
  • arcAngle sweeps counterclockwise when positive, clockwise when negative. Counterclockwise means upward on the screen — this is the one place where the angles behave the way your maths classes trained you, in spite of y pointing down.

So drawArc(50, 50, 200, 200, 0, 90) draws the top-right quarter of a circle, and drawArc(50, 50, 200, 200, 0, -90) draws the bottom-right quarter. fillArc closes the arc back to the center and fills it, giving you a pie slice.

Quick Check 19.5

Give the exact call for each. Assume you have a Graphics g and a panel 400 wide by 300 tall.

(a) A solid circle of diameter 80 centered in the panel.

The center is at (200, 150), and a circle of diameter 80 needs its bounding box to start 40 to the left and 40 above:

g.fillOval(200 - 40, 150 - 40, 80, 80);
// g.fillOval(160, 110, 80, 80);

Written so that it survives a resize:

int d = 80;
g.fillOval(getWidth() / 2 - d / 2, getHeight() / 2 - d / 2, d, d);

(b) A rectangle outline occupying the whole left half of the panel.

g.drawRect(0, 0, 200, 300); — or, resize-proof, g.drawRect(0, 0, getWidth() / 2, getHeight());

Note that the right-hand edge and the bottom edge of the outline land on x = 200 and y = 300, and y = 300 is one past the last row of a 300-tall panel, so the bottom edge is not visible. drawRect(0, 0, getWidth() - 1, getHeight() - 1) is the call that puts a visible border all the way round a component.

(c) A horizontal line right across the middle.

g.drawLine(0, 150, 399, 150); — the two endpoints, not a start and a length. drawLine is the only one of these methods whose last two arguments are a position rather than a size, and mixing that up is worth a mark on an exam.

(d) The word Total starting 10 pixels in from the left edge and 20 pixels down from the top.

g.drawString("Total", 10, 20); — and be aware that because 20 is the baseline, the letters occupy roughly y 8 to 20 rather than 20 to 32. If you want text comfortably clear of the top edge, give it a y of at least the font size, not a y of 2.

Things to notice across all four parts. Three of the four methods take a position and a size; drawLine takes two positions; drawString takes the text first and then a position that means something different again. When you are unsure, the reliable question is "does the last pair of numbers describe how big it is, or where it ends?"

Worked Example— BasicDrawingDemo, every method at once

This is your instructor's tour of the drawing methods. It is split into two files: a JPanel subclass that does nothing but draw, and a JFrame that puts one on the screen. That split is worth copying — the panel is now a class you can reuse, test, or drop into any window.

DrawingPanel.java

import java.awt.BasicStroke;
import java.awt.Color;
import java.awt.Font;
import java.awt.Graphics;
import java.awt.Graphics2D;

import javax.swing.JPanel;

public class DrawingPanel extends JPanel {

   protected void paintComponent(Graphics g) {
      super.paintComponent(g);

      // Cast to Graphics2D for advanced features
      Graphics2D g2d = (Graphics2D) g;

      g.setColor(Color.BLACK);

      g.setColor(Color.RED);

4.Color, and a word about Font

A Graphics has a current color. Every drawing method uses it, and it stays set until you change it. There is no color argument on any of the methods in §3, which is why your instructor's code is a strict alternation of setColor and a drawing call.

Definition— Color

java.awt.Color represents a color as three numbers — the amounts of red, green and blue light mixed together — each from 0 to 255. There are three ways to get one:

  • A named constant: Color.RED, Color.BLUE, Color.GREEN, Color.BLACK, Color.WHITE, Color.YELLOW, Color.ORANGE, Color.PINK, Color.CYAN, Color.MAGENTA, Color.GRAY, Color.LIGHT_GRAY, Color.DARK_GRAY.
  • new Color(r, g, b) with three numbers of your own.
  • new Color(rgb) with a single int holding all three packed together — red in the top byte, then green, then blue. This is the form your instructor uses to make random colors.

g.setColor(c) makes c the current color. A Color object never changes once created; to change color you set a different one.

The constants are ordinary Color objects and you can ask them their components. Two of the answers below are not what people guess. Predict Color.ORANGE and Color.PINK before you reveal.

5.repaint(): how you ask for a redraw

You know where the drawing code goes and you know you must not call it. So how does a picture ever change in response to a button click?

Definition— repaint

repaint() is a method on every component. It does not draw anything. It puts a request on a queue: "this component is out of date, please paint it when convenient". Swing collects those requests, works out what actually needs redrawing, and later calls paintComponent on the event dispatch thread.

The pattern is always the same two steps:

  1. Change the data the panel draws from — a field, a list, a coordinate.
  2. Call repaint() on the panel.

paintComponent then reads the new data and produces the new picture.

Key Idea— why not just call paintComponent yourself?

Three reasons, in increasing order of importance.

  1. You do not have a Graphics to give it. The only valid one is the one Swing hands to paintComponent. Getting one another way is the mistake §6 is about.
  2. You would skip half the work. paintComponent draws the content; the border and any child components are painted by paintBorder and paintChildren afterwards, and calling the middle one on its own gets you a component drawn without its border.
  3. Ten changes should cost one repaint. If a loop moves twenty shapes and calls repaint() twenty times, Swing collapses those requests and repaints once. Twenty direct calls to paintComponent would be twenty full redraws, and the user would see every intermediate state flicker past.

Say it as a rule: repaint() is a request; paintComponent is the response. You only ever write the request.

Quick Check 19.9

A panel has a field private int circleX = 50; and a paintComponent that draws a circle at circleX. A button's actionPerformed contains this:

   circleX = circleX + 20;

Plan. The data changed. Ask who was told.

Things to notice. The listener changes data and asks for a repaint; it never draws. That division is the whole architecture of a Swing graphics program, and it is what §7 formalises.

An interesting confirmation: without the repaint() line, minimising the window and restoring it makes the circle jump to its correct new position. The uncover forced a paint, and paintComponent read the field that had been changing all along. If a student ever reports that behavior, the diagnosis is certain.

6.Direct drawing versus painting

There is another way to get a Graphics, and your instructor's notes show it deliberately so that you can watch it fail. It is called direct drawing.

Definition— direct drawing

Direct drawing means asking a component for a Graphics yourself, with Graphics g = component.getGraphics();, and drawing on it from ordinary code — a listener, a loop, anywhere.

It has an immediate visual effect, and it is not persistent. The pixels you set are the ones on the screen right now; nothing remembers them. The next time Swing repaints that component, for any reason, it calls paintComponent, which knows nothing about your drawing, and your picture is gone.

Painting means putting the drawing in paintComponent and calling repaint(). It is persistent, because the picture is rebuilt from data every time it is needed. For everything you write in this course, painting is the right answer.

Quick Check 19.10

Two ways to make an area of a window red: panel.setBackground(Color.RED); and, inside paintComponent, g.setColor(Color.RED); g.fillRect(0, 0, getWidth(), getHeight());

(a) Which one survives the window being covered and uncovered?

Both do — and that is the point of the question. setBackground stores a property on the component, and super.paintComponent(g) re-fills with it on every repaint. The fillRect version is inside paintComponent, so it runs on every repaint too. Neither is direct drawing.

The version that would not survive is panel.getGraphics().fillRect(...) from a listener.

(b) Your GUI exam questions ask you to make a panel red. Which one do they expect?

setBackground. Every GUI question on the CSCI 212 exams that involves color — the red error window, the panels that swap colors when a button is clicked, the center panel that turns red, orange or green — is solved with setBackground(Color.RED) on a component, not with a drawing method.

That is worth knowing about the exam: colored areas are components with a background; fillRect is for shapes you are drawing yourself.

(c) Give one thing the fillRect version can do that setBackground cannot.

Anything that is not the whole rectangle: half the panel, a stripe, a circle, a gradient of a hundred rectangles, a shape whose size depends on data. setBackground colors the entire component and nothing else.

Things to notice across all three parts. The choice is not "drawing versus components" as a matter of taste. If the thing you want is a rectangle that fills a region and reacts to clicks, make it a component and set its background; Swing will manage it. If it is a picture, draw it in paintComponent. Reaching for graphics when a component would do is how people end up reimplementing buttons.

7.A panel that remembers what to draw

The fix for GraphicsDemo is not a better drawing routine. It is a change of mind about what the program is. Right now the picture lives on the screen. It has to live in the program instead, as data, and the screen has to become a report of that data.

Key Idea— the picture is data, not pixels

Keep a list of the things that should be on the screen. paintComponent draws every item in the list, from scratch, every time it is called. A user action adds to the list, removes from it, or edits it — and then calls repaint().

Now every one of the events that used to destroy the picture — a resize, an uncover, a minimise — simply causes the list to be drawn again. The picture is indestructible because it was never really on the screen in the first place.

Definition— Model, View, Controller

This three-way split has a name. MVC divides a program into:

  • the model — the data: the Shape class and the ArrayList;
  • the view — the code that displays it: paintComponent;
  • the controller — the code that handles user input: actionPerformed.

The point is that each part can be changed without disturbing the others. Add a new shape type and only the model and the view change. Replace the menus with buttons and only the controller changes. Add a "save to file" feature and only the model is involved, because the model is the only place the picture actually exists.

Common Mistake— a class called Shape collides with java.awt.Shape

java.awt already contains an interface named Shape. PaintingPanel gets away with import java.awt.*; because a class in your own directory beats a wildcard import. Add an explicit import java.awt.Shape; — which an IDE will cheerfully offer to do for you — and everything falls apart at once:

8.Letting each shape draw itself

Look again at the loop in paintComponent:

for (int i = 0; i < data.size(); i++) {
   Shape s = data.get(i);
   g.setColor(new Color(s.rgbValue));
   String command = s.shapeType;
   if (command.equals("Circle")) g.fillOval(s.x, s.y, s.size, s.size);
   if (command.equals("Rectangle")) g.fillRect(s.x, s.y, s.size, s.size);
   if (command.equals("Line")) { ... }
}

A heterogeneous collection, walked in a loop, with a chain of tests asking each element "what kind of thing are you?" so that the right code can be chosen. Lecture 13 had a name for that shape of code, and a replacement for it.

Key Idea— the payoff for Lectures 12, 13 and 14

Instead of a String saying what kind of shape it is, make each kind a subclass. Give the superclass one abstract method, draw(Graphics g), and let every subclass implement it its own way. Then the loop becomes one line:

for (int i = 0; i < data.size(); i++) {
   data.get(i).draw(g);
}

and dynamic binding picks the right draw for each element at run time. The panel no longer knows that circles exist. Adding a triangle means writing one new class and touching nothing that already works.

Quick Check 19.12

Questions about the polymorphic design.

(a) Shape is declared abstract. What exactly does the compiler refuse to let you write, and why is that a good thing here?

It refuses new Shape(Color.RED, 10, 10, 50) — error: Shape is abstract; cannot be instantiated. That is right, because a plain shape has no meaning: there would be no answer to the question of what draw should do. Declaring the class abstract turns a design decision into a rule the compiler enforces.

You can still write Shape s = new Circle(...); and hold shapes in an ArrayList<Shape>, which is the whole point — abstract classes cannot be instantiated but they can absolutely be used as types.

(b) PaintingPanel.paintComponent calls data.get(i).draw(g). The static type of data.get(i) is Shape, and Shape.draw has no body. What runs?

The draw of whatever class the object actually is — Circle.draw, Square.draw or Diagonal.draw. The declared type decides what you are allowed to call; the object decides what actually runs. That is dynamic binding, unchanged from Lecture 13.

The abstract declaration is what makes the call legal in the first place: the compiler needs to see a draw on Shape to accept the line, and the abstract method is that promise.

(c) Compare this to Quick Check 19.11: how many files change when you add a Triangle?

One new file, Triangle.java, plus two small edits in PaintingDemo — the menu name and one line in randomShape. Shape does not change. PaintingPanel does not change at all, and neither does Circle, Square or Diagonal.

In the string-tag version you had to reach into a method that already worked, in the class that draws everything, and add a case to it. Here you add a class. Nothing that was working can break, because nothing that was working was edited.

Things to notice across all three parts. This is the argument for polymorphism in its most concrete form, and it is worth being able to make it in a sentence on an exam: a chain of if statements asking what type an object is can almost always be replaced by an abstract method, and the version with the abstract method grows by addition instead of by editing.

Blue. The blue rectangle runs from (100, 80) for 200 across and 100 down, so x 100 to 299 and y 80 to 179. (280, 170) is inside it; the red one ended at x = 249 and y = 149.

(d) (250, 50)

Black. This is the one to think about. fillRect(50, 50, 200, 100) fills up to but not including x = 250, so the red does not reach here. But drawRect(50, 50, 200, 100) puts its outline on x = 50 + 200 = 250, and it was drawn last. The outline of a drawRect is one pixel wider than the fill of the corresponding fillRect, which is the fact §3 measured.

(e) (250, 150)

Black. The bottom-right corner of the same outline: x = 50 + 200 and y = 50 + 100. The blue rectangle covers this point too, but the outline was drawn after it.

(f) (30, 30)

White — the panel's background, put there by super.paintComponent(g). Nothing was drawn above y = 50 or left of x = 50.

Verified by painting the panel into an image and reading the pixels back:

(a)  ( 60,  60): RED
(b)  (150, 100): BLUE
(c)  (280, 170): BLUE
(d)  (250,  50): BLACK
(e)  (250, 150): BLACK
(f)  ( 30,  30): background

Things to notice across all six parts. Two rules answer every question of this kind, and they are worth writing at the top of your exam paper. A fill covers x to x + w - 1; an outline is drawn on x and on x + w. And the last thing drawn is the thing you see.

The reliable technique is to sketch the rectangles on paper with their real ranges written beside them — "red: 50–249 by 50–149" — and then read off each point. Trying to hold three overlapping rectangles in your head is how this question gets missed.

Problem 4 — A target (added for these notes)

Write a complete class TargetPanel that extends JPanel and draws an archery target: five concentric filled circles centered in the panel, alternating red and white, with the outermost 150 pixels across and each one inside it 30 pixels smaller. The target must stay centered when the window is resized. Use two named constants for the number of rings and the step.

Plan. Concentric circles are a fill-order problem: draw the largest first and let each smaller one cover the middle of the last. Drawing smallest first would hide everything under the biggest circle.

Ring i (counting from 0) has diameter (RINGS - i) * STEP, so ring 0 is 5 × 30 = 150 across and ring 4 is 30. Each is centered, so its bounding box starts at cx - diameter/2. Alternate the color with i % 2.

TargetPanel.java

import java.awt.Color;
import java.awt.Graphics;

import javax.swing.JPanel;

public class TargetPanel extends JPanel {
   public static final int RINGS = 5;      // number of colored bands
   public static final int STEP  = 30;     // each ring is 30 pixels wider than the next

   public void paintComponent(Graphics g) {
      super.paintComponent(g);

      int cx = getWidth() / 2;             // center of the panel
      int cy = getHeight() / 2;

      for (int i = 0; i < RINGS; i++) {
         int diameter = (RINGS - i) * STEP;
         if (i % 2 == 0) g.setColor(Color.RED);
         else            g.setColor(Color.WHITE);
         g.fillOval(cx - diameter / 2, cy - diameter / 2, diameter, diameter);
      }
   }
}

Checked on a 300 by 240 panel with a light gray background, so that "outside the target" is distinguishable from a white ring. The center is at (150, 120), and the ring radii are 75, 60, 45, 30 and 15:

panel is 300 x 240, so the center is (150, 120)
   dead center       (150, 120): red
   20 px right       (170, 120): white
   35 px right       (185, 120): red
   50 px right       (200, 120): white
   70 px right       (220, 120): red
   90 px right       (240, 120): panel background
   40 px straight up (150,  80): red

Red, white, red, white, red as you walk outward, and beyond 75 pixels you are off the target. The last probe checks the other axis: 40 pixels straight up is the same distance from the center as 40 to the right, so it must be the same color, and it is.

Things to notice. RINGS is odd on purpose. With an even number the innermost circle would be white and the bullseye would vanish into the background — which is exactly the kind of off-by-one you can only find by drawing it.

getWidth() and getHeight() are read inside paintComponent, not stored in fields. That is what makes it survive a resize: the panel's size at the moment of painting is the size that gets used, and Swing repaints after every resize.

An alternative with no alternating colors at all: draw the biggest circle red, then use drawOval in white for the ring boundaries. It is fewer pixels of work, but it gives you thin white lines rather than thick bands. Which one is right depends on the picture you want; both are correct programs.

Problem 5 — Short answer (added for these notes)

Six one-or-two-sentence questions of the kind that open a graphics problem.

(a) Where is the origin of a component's coordinate system, and which way does y increase?

At the top-left corner of the component; x increases to the right and y increases downward. So a larger y means further down the screen, the opposite of the mathematical convention.

(b) Name the two rules about paintComponent that are most often broken.

Never call it yourself — call repaint() and let Swing call it. And make super.paintComponent(g); its first statement, so the component is cleared before you redraw.

(c) What do the four arguments of drawOval(x, y, w, h) mean?

They are the bounding box: (x, y) is the top-left corner of the rectangle the ellipse is inscribed in, and w and h are that rectangle's width and height. They are not a center and a radius. For a circle of diameter d centered at (cx, cy) the call is drawOval(cx - d/2, cy - d/2, d, d).

(d) What does the y in drawString(s, x, y) refer to?

The baseline of the text — the line the letters sit on. The characters are drawn above that y, with descenders dipping below it, so drawString("Hi", 0, 0) puts the text off the top of the component and you see nothing.

(e) Give one advantage of painting over direct drawing, in one sentence.

A painted picture is rebuilt from data every time the component is redrawn, so it survives resizing, minimising, and being covered by another window — a directly drawn one does not.

Half a mark for "and it can be saved, printed or undone, because the picture exists as objects rather than as pixels".

(f) A Graphics reference g needs setStroke, which Graphics does not have. Write the line that fixes it and say why it is safe.

Graphics2D g2 = (Graphics2D) g; — a downcast. It is safe because the object Swing passes to paintComponent is always really a Graphics2D; the parameter is only declared as Graphics for historical reasons, so the cast is checking something that is already true.

Every one of these is a single sentence from §1 to §6 turned into a question, and each is the sort of thing that decides whether the rest of your answer can score. If you can write (a), (b) and (c) from memory, you have the three facts that the other errors in this lecture are all downstream of.

Lecture Summary
  • The origin (0, 0) is the top-left corner of the component, x increases to the right, and y increases downward. "Higher up the screen" means a smaller y.
  • Drawing happens in paintComponent(Graphics g), which you override on a JPanel subclass and never call. Its first line must be super.paintComponent(g);, which clears the component — leave it out and old drawings stay underneath.
  • Swing calls paintComponent on first display, on resize, when the component is uncovered, and when you call repaint(). It must be able to rebuild the whole picture every time.
  • repaint() is a request; paintComponent is the response. Change the data, then call repaint() on the panel.
  • drawRect, drawOval, drawRoundRect and drawArc all take a bounding box: top-left corner, width, height — never a center and a radius. drawLine is the exception; its last two numbers are a second point. drawString(s, x, y) puts the text's baseline at y, so the letters sit above it.
  • A Graphics carries a current color and font, set with setColor and setFont and used by every drawing call until changed. Colors come from constants such as Color.RED or from new Color(r, g, b).
  • Direct drawing through getGraphics() puts pixels on the screen and nothing remembers them; the picture is lost on the next repaint. Painting keeps the picture as data and redraws it on demand. Always paint.
  • The design that makes it work is MVC: a list of Shape objects (the model), a paintComponent that draws them (the view), and an actionPerformed that edits the list and calls repaint() (the controller).
  • Give Shape an abstract draw(Graphics g) and let every subclass implement it, and the panel's chain of if (type.equals(...)) collapses into data.get(i).draw(g). Adding a shape becomes writing a new class instead of editing a working one — the whole argument for Lectures 12 to 14, in twenty lines.
